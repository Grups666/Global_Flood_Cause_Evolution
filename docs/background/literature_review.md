# Literature basis: changes in flood-generating processes

## Why the analysis is event- and process-specific

The project asks whether the *processes generating large rainfall-driven floods* have changed within observed catchments. This is different from asking only whether annual flood peaks changed. A catchment can produce large floods through several combinations of rainfall organization and antecedent wetness. Pooling those events can cancel opposing changes.

The literature supports four design choices:

1. identify hydrological events before selecting extremes;
2. select extremes using streamflow, then characterize their drivers;
3. classify each event rather than assigning one permanent process to a catchment;
4. examine both the frequency of each process and the magnitude of the floods it produces.

## Core papers named in the meeting

### Stein, Pianosi and Woods (2020)

**Event-based classification for global study of river flood generating processes.** *Hydrological Processes*, 34, 1514–1529. <https://doi.org/10.1002/hyp.13678>

This global study classified 113,635 flood events in 4,155 catchments into process types. Its most important result for this project is that within-catchment process variability is often high: one dominant label is insufficient for many catchments, and the most extreme event can be generated by a different process from the most frequent one. This directly supports retaining an event-level process time series.

### Stein, Clark, Knoben, Pianosi and Woods (2021)

**How do climate and catchment attributes influence flood generating processes? A large-sample study for 671 catchments across the contiguous USA.** *Water Resources Research*, 57, e2020WR028300. <https://doi.org/10.1029/2020WR028300>

The study shows that process mixtures vary systematically with climate and catchment properties. It also cautions against treating a visually dense gauge network as a spatially uniform sample. For the present project, catchments remain the inferential units; no ungauged-area extrapolation is made.

### Tarasova et al. (2019)

**Causative classification of river flood events.** *WIREs Water*, 6, e1353. <https://doi.org/10.1002/wat2.1353>

This review distinguishes causative classifications from purely seasonal or hydrograph-shape groupings. It recommends testing whether proposed process types have different runoff responses, and it notes that peak-over-threshold sampling can provide more events per process than annual maxima. It also warns that classifications based on absolute thresholds can be catchment-dependent and threshold-sensitive.

### Tarasova et al. (2020a)

**A process-based framework to characterize and classify runoff events: the event typology of Germany.** *Water Resources Research*, 56, e2019WR026951. <https://doi.org/10.1029/2019WR026951>

This paper provides the operational rainfall-organization rule used here. For daily data, a rainfall event is intensity-dominated when both

\[
CV_t(P)>1
\quad\text{and}\quad
\frac{P_{\max}}{P_{\mathrm{event}}}>0.5.
\]

Otherwise it is volume-dominated. The two conditions distinguish a genuinely peaked temporal pattern from a merely high ratio caused by a short or sparse event. The paper uses antecedent soil moisture to distinguish wet and dry event states and demonstrates that event types have different hydrological responses.

### Tarasova, Basso and Merz (2020b)

**Transformation of generation processes from small runoff events to large floods.** *Geophysical Research Letters*, 47, e2020GL090547. <https://doi.org/10.1029/2020GL090547>

Using 203,852 events in 172 German catchments, this study shows that the process mixture can change between ordinary events, annual floods and upper-tail floods. Rare intensity-dominated events on dry soils can become important only in the upper tail. This is why the present study selects the large-flood sample first and then studies its internal process composition.

### Tarasova et al. (2023)

**Shifts in flood generation processes exacerbate regional flood anomalies in Europe.** *Communications Earth & Environment*, 4, 49. <https://doi.org/10.1038/s43247-023-00714-8>

This is the closest precedent for the temporal question. It estimates catchment-wise changes in the frequency of classified flood processes with Sen slopes and Mann–Kendall tests. It requires at least five events of a process as a data-availability/robustness compromise. It also compares flood-magnitude distributions across processes. The present experiment therefore uses five process events as its single process-specific minimum and reports process frequency together with flood response.

## Closely related context

- Berghuijs et al. (2016), **Dominant flood generating mechanisms across the United States**, shows why rainfall amount alone cannot explain US floods: antecedent wetness and snow processes matter. <https://doi.org/10.1002/2016GL068070>
- Berghuijs et al. (2019), **The relative importance of different flood-generating mechanisms across Europe**, provides a continental comparison of process importance. <https://doi.org/10.1029/2019WR024841>
- Jiang, Bevacqua and Zscheischler (2022), **River flooding mechanisms and their changes in Europe revealed by explainable machine learning**, finds event-level shifts even when a catchment does not switch cleanly from one dominant mechanism to another. <https://doi.org/10.5194/hess-26-6339-2022>
- Giani et al. (2022), **An objective time-series-based method for rainfall-runoff event identification**, supports explicit, reproducible event separation before extreme-value selection. <https://doi.org/10.1029/2021WR031283>

## What is adopted, adapted and not claimed

**Adopted from the literature:** event-level classification; the joint `CV > 1` and `Pmax/Pevent > 0.5` rainfall-organization rule; a five-event process minimum; Sen-slope/Mann–Kendall trend estimation; Q90, Q97.5 and annual-maximum sensitivity samples.

**Adapted to the available global data:** antecedent wetness uses daily SSI on the day before rainfall starts. `Dry`, `Moderate` and `Wet` are defined by recalculated pooled 1/3 and 2/3 quantiles of all valid daily SSI values throughout 1982–2019 in the primary-sample catchments, including non-flood days. The resulting 0.404690 and 0.576339 cutoffs are dataset-derived, not numerical constants borrowed from Tarasova et al. Retaining the middle category avoids forcing intermediate states into a binary split. Wetness cut-point and antecedent-window sensitivities are reported separately. The analysis is limited to rainfall-driven, low-snow catchments and the verified common record.

**Not claimed:** classification proves causal attribution; a trend in a driver proves it caused a flood-magnitude trend; points between gauges represent ungauged catchments; or a single global direction exists. The output identifies observed catchments where process occurrence, process conditions or associated flood response changed consistently through time.

## How the papers determine the experiment

| Evidence from the literature | Consequence for this project |
|---|---|
| Stein, Pianosi and Woods show large within-catchment process diversity | Classify individual events; never assign one permanent mechanism to a catchment |
| Tarasova et al. distinguish causative classes from seasonal or hydrograph-only classes | Use rainfall organization and antecedent wetness as generating conditions |
| The German event typology requires both a high rainiest-day fraction and high temporal CV | Use the joint \(P_{max}/P_{event}>0.5\) and \(CV_t>1\) intensity rule |
| Upper-tail process mixtures differ from ordinary-event mixtures | Select large floods first and analyze the mechanisms inside that selected tail |
| European shift analysis studies process occurrence through time | Estimate annual process frequency and process share, rather than only trends in the continuous drivers |
| Process types have different runoff responses | Estimate direct stormflow volume and daily flood peak within each process |
| POT supplies more events of uncommon processes than annual maxima | Use catchment-specific Q95 as the primary sample and annual maxima as a sensitivity sample |

## Decisions that come from the meeting or available data

The literature does not by itself determine every parameter. The 2 September meeting specified that the primary upper tail should be ranked by reconstructed **event stormflow volume** and that the first result should describe what happened to the selected floods. The verified data determine the common 1982–2019 period and the low-snow restriction. The three antecedent states use the audited categories already attached to the source events. These choices are therefore documented separately from the published intensity rule and the five-event process minimum.
